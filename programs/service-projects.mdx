Every Tuesday evening, Providence Road youth lace up and show up. They move through North Baton Rouge neighborhoods with trash bags, gloves, and the expectation that the block they just cleaned is their block — and that matters. What looks like a cleanup project from the outside is actually something far more deliberate: a weekly practice ground for teamwork, consistency, and the kind of loyalty that only comes from doing hard things side by side. Since 2018, that steady Tuesday commitment has added up to an estimated **75 tons of trash and debris removed from the inner city of Baton Rouge**.

## What Happens on a Tuesday

Service projects are the connective tissue of the Providence Road model. Youth gather in the neighborhoods where they live and fan out — picking up litter along roadsides, clearing overgrown and abandoned vacant lots, hauling away debris that has accumulated for years. Staff and volunteers work shoulder to shoulder with the youth. Nobody watches from the sideline.

The structure isn't just practical. It's relational. Picking up trash gives youth a shared aim, a visible result, and a reason to come back next week. It's also where the culture of "strong over tough" gets practiced in real time — not talked about, but lived out.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Gather and brief">
    Youth and volunteers meet at the designated neighborhood location. The week's area is assigned and the top-performer system is reviewed — the five hardest workers earn a seat in the truck and access to additional opportunities.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Work the block">
    The crew fans out to pick up trash, pull debris, and clear vacant lots. Staff circulate, encourage, and hold the standard.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Haul and measure">
    Bags are collected, counted, and removed. Results are tracked — the numbers are real and the youth know it.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Celebrate the effort">
    Top performers are recognized. The group debrief reinforces what strong looks like and what next week holds.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Capitol Lake: A Partnership That Went to the Capitol

One of the most significant chapters in Providence Road's service story began when Coach J' learned about the work Marie Constantin and the **Louisiana Stormwater Coalition** were doing to reclaim Capitol Lake — a body of water that had been neglected for decades while storm drains dumped an average of 41 bags of litter per month onto its shores.

The two organizations recognized each other immediately. Both were doing the same thing in different arenas: showing up for something neglected and believing it was worth reclaiming.

> "To date, our guys, working with Ms. Marie, have rolled out 328 tires, removed two toilets, a couch, numerous large items, 50 bags of litter, and hauled two boats up out of a ravine."
>
> — Cole Erno, Director of Programs, Providence Road

Week after week, Providence Road youth joined the Coalition's cleanup of Capitol Lake and its surrounding watershed. The partnership grew into something bigger than trash removal.

On May 12th, youth from Providence Road joined Marie Constantin on a trip to the **State Legislature** to meet Senator Franklin Foil. The Louisiana Stormwater Coalition proposed a resolution to study funding sources for capturing litter from stormwater before it damages watersheds. Senator Foil sponsored the bill — and it passed.

These young men from North Baton Rouge, who had spent months pulling tires out of a lake, sat in a seat where decisions get made. That is what service as formation looks like.

## Why Service Is the Entry Point

Providence Road doesn't lead with lectures or life-skills worksheets. It leads with a trash bag and an invitation to show up on Tuesday. That's by design.

The Cultivation Model — Providence Road's five-stage engagement framework — begins with **engaging the community** and **building connection**. Service projects deliver both at once. They put youth in motion toward a common goal, create natural openings for conversation, and make it easy for adults to earn trust without demanding it.

The "seat in the truck" system reinforces this intentionality: the five youth who work hardest each week earn a spot for the next level of programming — gym trips, out-of-town service, and expanded mentorship. Distinction is something you earn through effort, not seniority.
