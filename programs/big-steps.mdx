Big Steps™ is a student-led, peer-affirmation practice developed by Providence Road that makes personal growth **visible**, **named**, and **valued**. Instead of rewarding compliance with points or prizes, it trains students to recognize their own effortful "firsts" and affirm one another authentically — building the kind of durable motivation and identity growth that no behavior chart can manufacture. Whether you run it in a morning advisory, a coaching huddle, or an after-school block, Big Steps™ installs a culture of care and ownership that spills into every other part of the school day.

<Note>
  Big Steps™ is secular and school-compatible. It was developed by Providence Road, a nonprofit serving at-risk youth in North Baton Rouge.
</Note>

## What Is Big Steps™?

Big Steps™ gives students a structured ritual for naming growth and receiving genuine peer recognition. At the heart of every session is the **N.V.I.T.E.S. Arc™** — a six-move sequence that guides students from noticing a change in themselves all the way through sharing that growth with others:

| Step | Move | What it means |
|------|------|---------------|
| **N** | Notice | Catch the moment of effort or change |
| **V** | Value | Affirm that the step actually mattered |
| **I** | Inquire | Ask curious, specific questions about it |
| **T** | Trade | Swap a shortcut or avoidance for honest effort |
| **E** | Enjoy | Sit with the partial or full win |
| **S** | Share | Pass the momentum to someone else |

The practice is student-run. Adults facilitate the first weeks, then gradually hand roles — timekeeper, affirmer, historian — to students themselves. The long-term goal is a group that can sustain Big Steps™ without adult prompting.

## What Counts as a "Big Step"?

A Big Step is any **meaningful, effort-based first** — not a huge life transformation, just a real departure from the habitual default. Examples include:

- Asking for help instead of shutting down
- Apologizing to a peer or teacher
- Turning every assignment in on time for a full week
- Stepping back from a conflict before it escalated
- Inviting someone new into a conversation or activity
- Sticking with a hard task past the point of wanting to quit
- Speaking up respectfully when something felt unfair

The emphasis on *firsts* matters. Big Steps™ doesn't reward students for doing something they already do with ease — it names the moment they did something differently, and makes that moment worth remembering.

## How a Session Runs

<Steps>
  <Step title="Name the Big Step">
    A student identifies a recent effort-based first and shares it with the group. Participation is always opt-in — students who aren't ready to share can still be present and affirming.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Peers Give Specific, Authentic Affirmation (Trail Mix Role™)">
    Other students respond using the **Trail Mix Role™** — offering affirmations that are specific, honest, and grounded in what they actually observed or know about that student. No generic praise. The goal is recognition that feels true.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Group Recalls Prior Steps">
    The group briefly revisits steps shared in earlier sessions. This builds **cultural memory** — the group's shared story of growth — and signals that steps don't disappear after the meeting ends.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Quick Responsibility Check">
    The facilitator (or a student leader) asks: *"Are you ready for the next step?"* This brief moment links past effort to future intention and keeps the practice forward-facing.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Close with One N.V.I.T.E.S. Action">
    The session ends with each student identifying one specific N.V.I.T.E.S. action they will carry into the rest of the day. This bridges the circle to real behavior outside the room.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Big Steps™ is especially effective with students who are easily distracted, avoidant, or "over school" — young people who have learned that effort leads to embarrassment rather than recognition. Mixed-ability groups work well because the practice values *any* effortful first, not academic performance.

## Why It Works

Big Steps™ isn't a motivation trick. It builds the psychological infrastructure that makes motivation possible. Each session trains:

<Accordion title="Growth Mindset">
  By naming and celebrating effortful firsts — rather than innate talent or results — Big Steps™ reinforces the belief that effort produces change. Students build a catalog of personal evidence that they are capable of doing hard things.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Social-Emotional Learning (SEL)">
  Empathy, belonging, and authentic communication are practiced every session. Peers learn to give affirmations that are true and specific, not just flattering — a skill that transfers into friendships, classrooms, and eventually workplaces.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Distress Tolerance">
  Sharing a vulnerable first in front of peers requires holding discomfort. The Trail Mix Role™ and opt-in structure make that risk safe, and students build tolerance for vulnerability over time.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Executive Function">
  The responsibility check and daily N.V.I.T.E.S. action train self-regulation: pause, assess, plan, act. Students practice linking present choices to future identity.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Sense of Purpose">
  When a step is named and remembered by a group, it becomes part of a student's story. Over weeks, students begin to see themselves as people who do hard things — and that identity shapes future behavior.
</Accordion>

## How It Differs from PBIS and Points Systems

<Accordion title="No tickets. No prizes. No compliance loop.">
  PBIS and points systems reward visible compliance — they work by attaching external incentives to desired behaviors. Big Steps™ does the opposite: it centers **peer recognition and identity growth**. There is no reward to chase and no ticket to lose. The only currency is the group's genuine regard, which turns out to be far more durable.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Builds motivation, doesn't just manage behavior.">
  External rewards tend to work as long as they're offered and stop working when they're removed. Big Steps™ trains students to find the reward inside the effort — in the recognition, the remembered step, and the identity it builds. That kind of motivation travels with students when the program ends.
</Accordion>

## Trauma-Informed Design

Big Steps™ was built with trauma-informed principles from the ground up.

- **Opt-in disclosure** — Students choose whether and what to share. No student is ever required to speak.
- **Student-controlled participation** — Students can receive affirmations without naming a step publicly. Written affirmations and small-group circles are always available.
- **Adult modeling** — Facilitators model consent, boundaries, and repair. Adults participate in the practice alongside students, not above them.
- **Privacy protection** — Steps are not posted publicly or shared outside the group without explicit student consent.

<Accordion title="What if a student hates attention?">
  Offer alternatives: small-group circles (4–5 students instead of the full group), written affirmations on cards or a shared doc, or one-to-one recognition with a trusted adult. Students never have to speak to be fully included in Big Steps™.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Does naming a 'first' reward bad past behavior?">
  No. Big Steps™ names what a student **did differently** — the prosocial, effortful action — not the problem that preceded it. The focus is always on what the student chose to do and how to repeat it. Facilitators are trained to keep the language forward-facing.
</Accordion>

## Timeline: When Will You See Change?

Most groups notice shifts **within 2–4 weeks** of consistent practice:

- More positive peer-to-peer language during and between sessions
- Fewer power struggles during transitions and independent work
- Students spontaneously naming and "stacking" their own steps outside the circle

Full cultural adoption — where students carry the language and ritual without adult prompting — typically takes one semester.

## What You Need to Get Started

<Steps>
  <Step title="Pick a Group and a Slot">
    Choose a group of 10–20 students and identify either a 10-minute daily slot or a 30-minute weekly slot. Consistency matters more than frequency.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Train Your Facilitators">
    Adult facilitators need a **2–3 hour onboarding** plus a practice circle, followed by 3–4 weeks of brief coaching huddles. Most staff feel confident by week two.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Run Big Steps™ for Four Weeks">
    Commit to four weeks before evaluating. Track a few light metrics: positive peer talk, participation rate, and student-named steps.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Review, Adjust, Expand">
    After four weeks, review what's working, adjust roles or prompts as needed, and consider expanding to additional groups or grade levels.
  </Step>
</Steps>

You'll need a **facilitator guide**, a simple **Step Log** (paper or digital), and a quiet corner. No special technology required.

## Frequently Asked Questions

<Accordion title="Can we customize Big Steps™ for our school?">
  Yes. Roles, prompts, and reflection tools can all be adapted for your context while keeping the core protocol intact: naming → affirmation → recall → readiness → N.V.I.T.E.S. action. Providence Road can support customization during the coaching phase.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="How does Big Steps™ fit with academic work?">
  It pairs naturally with ELA (personal narrative, reflection writing), advisory and SEL blocks, and coaching or athletics settings. Gains in focus, belonging, and self-regulation tend to spill over into coursework — teachers often notice the difference before they connect it to Big Steps™.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Can families be involved?">
  Yes. Send brief "step notes" home — a sentence or two about the step a student named that week. Invite families to add their own affirmations. Family recognition accelerates the momentum built in the group circle.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="What about student privacy?">
  Steps are not logged publicly or shared outside the group without student consent. Facilitators are trained to protect confidentiality and to avoid recording sensitive personal details in shared documents.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Is Big Steps™ religious?">
  No. Big Steps™ is fully secular and school-compatible. It was developed by Providence Road, a nonprofit serving youth in North Baton Rouge, but the practice carries no religious content or affiliation.
</Accordion>
