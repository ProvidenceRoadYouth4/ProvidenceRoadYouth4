Chasing Understanding® is a structured reading practice developed by Providence Road that teaches students to notice confusion, name it out loud, and work through it together. Most reading instruction focuses on what students understand — Chasing Understanding® focuses on the moment they *don't*, treating that gap not as failure but as the exact place where real learning happens. By making metacognition visible and socially safe, CU breaks the "fake read" habit that quietly drives disengagement across every grade level and content area. Whether you use it as a daily bell-ringer or a weekly deep dive, CU gives students a repeatable protocol for honest, effortful comprehension.

<Note>
  Chasing Understanding® is secular and school-compatible. Free starter kits are available from Providence Road, a nonprofit serving youth in North Baton Rouge.
</Note>

## What Is Chasing Understanding®?

Chasing Understanding® is built on a simple, countercultural premise: the moment you realize you *don't* understand something is the most valuable moment in a lesson — not a problem to hide, but a signal to chase. The practice gives students language and structure to act on that signal instead of skipping past it.

Like Big Steps™, CU uses the **N.V.I.T.E.S. Arc™** as its backbone:

| Step | Move | In a CU Session |
|------|------|-----------------|
| **N** | Notice | Catch the exact moment comprehension breaks down |
| **V** | Value | Name it: "This matters — the confusion is worth solving" |
| **I** | Inquire | Form a specific question about the confusion |
| **T** | Trade | Swap guessing or skipping for an honest fix-up strategy |
| **E** | Enjoy | Acknowledge the partial or full win of understanding more |
| **S** | Share | Tell the group what unlocked it — turning one student's breakthrough into everyone's |

CU is designed for real classrooms with real texts. Students use the same materials they'd encounter in a standard lesson — no special readers, no leveled substitutes. The practice changes what students *do with* confusion, not what they read.

## The Problem CU Solves

<Accordion title="The 'Fake Read' Problem">
  Most students who struggle with reading don't announce the problem — they perform reading. They move their eyes across the page, turn pages on schedule, and produce the surface-level behaviors of engagement while comprehension quietly fails. By the time a quiz or discussion reveals the gap, re-teaching takes far longer than the original lesson.

  CU interrupts that pattern at the source: the moment confusion first appears. When students learn to catch and name that moment instead of hiding it, real comprehension becomes possible — and teachers stop flying blind.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Why Metacognition Gets Skipped">
  Metacognition — thinking about your own thinking — is a high-leverage academic skill, but it rarely gets explicit instructional time. It's assumed, modeled briefly, and then expected. CU makes metacognition a daily practice with a consistent structure, so students build the habit rather than hoping it develops on its own.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="How CU Is Different from Regular Reading Instruction">
  Standard reading instruction focuses on products — answers, summaries, analyses. CU focuses on **the process of struggle**: the specific moves a reader makes when comprehension fails. Students practice naming confusion, forming better questions, and choosing more effective fix-up strategies. That skill set transfers across texts, subjects, and grade levels in ways that comprehension worksheets can't replicate.
</Accordion>

## How a CU Session Runs

<Steps>
  <Step title="Notice a Confusion Point">
    While reading, students actively watch for the moment comprehension breaks — an unfamiliar word, a confusing sentence structure, an unclear reference, a logic gap. Students mark it (sticky note, emoji code, shared doc) rather than skipping past it.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Value the Pause">
    The student or facilitator names the confusion aloud and affirms it: *"This matters — the pause is the right move."* This step is critical. It retrains the instinct to hide confusion and builds the cultural norm that not-knowing is worth voicing.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Inquire with Specific Questions">
    The student forms a specific question about the confusion point. Vague responses like "I don't get it" are replaced with focused questions like "I don't know what this word refers back to" or "I'm not sure why the author switched tense here." Question stems are posted in the room to support this step.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Trade the Shortcut for an Honest Attempt">
    Instead of guessing and moving on, the student tries a real fix-up strategy: going back to the text for evidence, asking a peer for a second read, or requesting a teacher cue. The "trade" is the swap of avoidance for effort — even a partial effort counts.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Enjoy the Partial Win">
    When understanding increases — even partially — the group names it. "I still don't know everything, but I know more than I did" is a real win. This step builds tolerance for ambiguity and prevents all-or-nothing thinking about comprehension.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Share What Unlocked It">
    The student who worked through the confusion shares what move or strategy helped — turning a personal breakthrough into a public resource. Over time, the group builds a shared library of fix-up strategies that belongs to everyone.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## The Four Core Behaviors CU Tracks

CU measures growth through four observable behaviors that deepen over time:

<Accordion title="1. Metacognitive Pause Recognition">
  **Did the student catch the moment?** Early on, students often pass confusion without noticing it. Over time, they begin to flag confusion points reliably — the moment of recognition becomes faster and more accurate.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="2. Confusion Articulation">
  **Is the confusion clear and specific?** The shift from "I don't get it" to "I don't understand why the author says one thing here but the opposite two paragraphs later" is measurable and teachable. CU tracks whether students' articulations become more precise over four-week cycles.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="3. Question Depth">
  **Are questions improving over time?** Surface questions ("What does this word mean?") give way to inferential and analytical questions ("Why does the author choose this word instead of a simpler one?"). CU Logs capture this progression.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="4. Resolution Source">
  **Where does the student go to resolve confusion?** The progression runs from guessing → skipping → rereading → using text evidence → asking a peer → using a strategy. CU tracks whether students are moving toward more reliable, text-grounded resolution moves.
</Accordion>

<Tip>
  Light indicators to watch alongside the four core behaviors: time-on-task during reading, frequency of rereads with evidence, and fewer "I don't know" shrugs during discussion.
</Tip>

## Trauma-Informed Design

CU was built with the understanding that many students who struggle with reading carry shame about that struggle. Every design choice is oriented toward reducing that shame while increasing honest effort.

- **Opt-in sharing** — Students mark confusion points silently before anything is shared with the group. No student is ever cold-called on a confusion they haven't chosen to voice.
- **Sentence stems** — Posted question stems reduce the barrier to articulating confusion. Students who can't yet form their own question can use a stem to get started.
- **Anonymous options** — Students can submit confusion points on sticky notes or through a shared doc without attaching their name. The question enters the group discussion without identifying its source.
- **Teacher modeling** — Facilitators regularly model their own confusion points in a text. Watching an adult say "I don't know what this means yet, and that's worth figuring out" is powerful permission.

<Accordion title="What if students are shy or anxious about sharing confusion?">
  Three options work well: mark confusion silently with a sticky note or emoji code, pair-share with one trusted peer before any whole-group sharing, or submit questions anonymously through a shared doc or paper slip. Students can fully participate in every step of the N.V.I.T.E.S. Arc™ without ever speaking to the whole group.
</Accordion>

## Timeline: When Will You See Change?

Most groups notice meaningful shifts **within 2–4 weeks** of consistent CU practice:

- More honest, specific questions during and after reading
- More frequent returns to the text for evidence
- Fewer "glaze-over" moments — students staying present longer during shared reading
- Students spontaneously naming confusion outside of CU sessions

Full fluency — where students apply the protocol independently without adult prompting — typically develops over one semester of consistent use.

## What You Need to Get Started

<Steps>
  <Step title="Pick One Class and a Slot">
    Start with one class period and identify a consistent 8–10 minute daily slot — typically the first minutes of class. Consistency in the early weeks builds the ritual before expanding to other groups.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Train Staff and Set Up the Room">
    Adult facilitators need a **2–3 hour onboarding** to learn the protocol, followed by brief weekly coaching huddles for the first month. Post question stems visibly, print CU Logs, and designate a space for sticky notes or a shared digital document.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Run CU for Four Weeks and Track the Four Core Behaviors">
    Commit to four consistent weeks before evaluating. Track the four core behaviors using CU Logs, and collect light indicators (time-on-task, evidence use, question quality).
  </Step>
  <Step title="Review, Adjust, Expand">
    After four weeks, review what the data shows. Adjust sentence stems, roles, or log format for your specific group, then expand to additional classes or subject areas.
  </Step>
</Steps>

You'll need a **common text** (print or digital), **sticky notes** or a shared doc, **posted sentence stems** for question-forming, and a **CU Log** to track confusion points and resolutions. No special curriculum purchase required.
