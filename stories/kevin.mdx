Kevin showed up at Coach J's truck one afternoon and said he wanted to join Providence Road. He was eleven years old. Before Coach J' could respond, one of the older high school leaders objected: *"Don't let him in Providence Road — it's his people who throw all the trash in the street and in the lot across the street."* Coach J' smiled and replied without hesitation: *"He is exactly who we want in Providence Road."* That moment — the welcome extended to the kid least expected to succeed — is the seed from which everything that followed grew.

## The Kid Who Kept Showing Up

Kevin became a Providence Road regular. Week after week, service project after service project, he showed up. Coach J' took note, recognizing something unique in Kevin's effort, but even he had no idea just how far Kevin would go.

Over time, Kevin made real progress. He learned to respond to conflict by using his words instead of his hands. He practiced getting a second opinion before reacting. Most importantly, he began to find his identity not in being the "tough guy" — the social currency the streets reward — but in being strong for others. The distinction matters to Providence Road: *tough is for yourself; strong is for others.* Kevin was learning to be strong.

The change rippled outward. Out of respect for Kevin's efforts, family members began leaving less trash in the street. A child's transformation was quietly reshaping his block.

> "He is the example of small choices adding up to huge and inviting others to join him."
>
> — Coach J' (Jon Odenwald)

## The Front Seat

Providence Road uses a simple but powerful incentive: youth who serve well earn a "seat in the truck" and the chance to be among the top five performers of the week. That seat opens doors to every other opportunity Providence Road offers. It reinforces that strength — not loudness, not toughness — is what earns recognition here.

During an ice storm in February, Kevin had one of his worst service days in months. Cold hands got the better of him, and he struggled to pull his weight. Young "D," on the other hand — a youth who often fluctuated in his consistency — brought his best effort and had one of his greatest service days ever.

After the project, the group gathered for their meeting. What happened next had never happened in Providence Road history.

Kevin, with no prompting from any adult, turned to Young D and said:

*"D, you did your business during the service project; you can have the front seat in the truck."*

Kevin held loosely to the prize he had earned through months of great performance and handed it to the person who deserved it that day. That is not acceptable behavior coached into compliance — that is a radical shift in character. It is how cultural norms change. Strong follows strong.

## Camp Blessing and Beyond

Kevin's story didn't stay in Baton Rouge. That summer, he and Jarroid (J') traveled to Camp Blessing in Brenham, Texas — an award-winning Christian camp ministry for children and adults with special needs — to serve for five days as part of the dining hall crew.

At first, neither youth was sold on the experience. Kevin complained about the shared lodging. But once the work started, something shifted. Midway through the week, Kevin walked up to Cole Erno and said, *"Hey Cole, I don't want to switch cabins anymore."*

By the end of the week, camp leaders had one thing to say about Kevin and J': they were the hardest workers they had seen all summer.

## What Kevin Represents

Kevin isn't the toughest or the loudest in the group. He never was. But he is often the most remarkable — a front-seat person at eleven years old who grew into someone the entire community watches. His journey from the kid other youth didn't want admitted to the kid inviting others into strength is not a program outcome. It is a person choosing, one small decision at a time, to become someone different.

That is the real goal of Providence Road: not compliance, not performance, but the unlocking of potential that changes how a young person does relationships, serves their community, and charts the trajectory of their life.
